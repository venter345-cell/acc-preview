Aviation medical deferred or not issued: what happens next?

What a deferral means, how SACAA reviews a case, limitations and shorter validity, the 30 day appeal, and what you must report between medicals.

By Dr A.O. Ndhlovu, MBChB (UCT), DOHM (UP), Cert. Avmed (SMU), SACAA Designated Aviation Medical Examiner (MR 336)

Draft prepared 29 October 2026Last updated 29 October 20264 min readFor review by Dr A.O. Ndhlovu

Quick answer

A deferral is not a final "no". Under Part 67 a designated examiner may defer your certificate until SACAA gives an instruction, usually once a specialist report or extra test is in. Complicated cases go to the SACAA Aeromedical Committee. A certificate can also be issued with a limitation or a shorter validity, and you may appeal a decision within 30 days.

Most aviation medicals end with a certificate on the day. Sometimes the examiner needs more information first. That can be worrying, especially if your training, your job or a check ride depends on it. This guide explains, step by step, what the regulations say happens next, so you know what to expect and what you can do.

What does a deferred medical mean?

Part 67, regulation 67.00.4, says a designated examiner may examine you, issue your certificate, or defer it pending an instruction from SACAA. A deferral means the examiner cannot decide on the day, usually because a finding or something in your history needs more information. It is not the same as being declared unfit.

The SACAA medical protocols list, condition by condition, the reports SACAA wants. Many ask for a recent specialist report, often not older than three months, and some ask for a test such as a stress ECG or a lung function test.

What happens after a deferral?

  1. The examiner explains what is needed, for example a specialist report, a test result or records from your own doctor.
  2. You arrange the report or test and send it to the examiner.
  3. Straightforward cases can then be finalised. SACAA procedure AM007 says complicated cases are referred to the SACAA Aeromedical Committee (AMC), an advisory panel of medical and aviation industry experts.
  4. The AMC meets on the third Tuesday of each month, and documents must be submitted at least seven days before the meeting.

Time matters here. Regulation 67.00.12 says the records of a medical examination may be used to issue a certificate for no more than 90 days. If your reports take longer than that, the examination may have to be repeated.

Can I get a certificate with a limitation?

Yes. Part 67 allows a certificate to be endorsed with a requirement or limitation when safe flying depends on it, for example wearing corrective lenses. Regulation 67.00.6(5) also lets the examiner or SACAA's medical assessor shorten the validity period, for instance so that a condition is reviewed more often.

Regulation 67.00.8(4A) goes further. In exceptional circumstances SACAA's medical assessor may issue or renew a certificate for someone who does not meet some of the standards, if accredited medical conclusion shows that flying is not likely to jeopardise safety, taking account of the person's ability, skill, experience and operating conditions. Any limitation that safety depends on is endorsed on the licence.

Can I appeal a decision?

Yes. SACAA procedure AM007 says applicants who are aggrieved by the decision of a designated examiner, an authorised officer or the designated body may appeal to the Director of Civil Aviation within 30 days, as prescribed in Part 67. If SACAA suspends a certificate, regulation 67.00.14 sets a shorter period: an appeal within 14 days of the suspension.

What must I report between medicals?

Your duties do not stop at the examination. Regulation 67.00.9 says you may not act as a pilot, air traffic service member or cabin crew member while you know of a medical condition or medicine that could affect your certificate, or once it has expired. You must also tell SACAA without undue delay about any:

  • Injury or hospital stay
  • Operation or invasive procedure
  • Regular use of medicine
  • Pregnancy
  • Illness that keeps you off duty for more than 21 days
  • Psychiatric treatment

That applies where the event means you no longer meet the medical standards. Before you fly again you must give SACAA proof that you have fully recovered. If you are unsure whether something counts, ask your examiner. AM007 says every applicant is responsible for consulting their DAME about medical conditions or medicines being considered, to make sure they are compatible with flying.

How to keep a deferral short

  • Tell us about any condition, operation or regular medicine when you book, so you can bring the right reports on the day.
  • Bring recent reports from your doctor or specialist, not only a referral letter.
  • Answer the history questions on the SACAA form fully. You sign a declaration that they are accurate, complete and true.
  • Keep copies of everything you send, and note the AMC dates if your case is referred.

Questions and answers

Does a deferred medical mean I failed?

+

No. A deferral means the examiner needs more information, such as a specialist report or a test, before the certificate can be issued. SACAA then decides, sometimes with advice from its Aeromedical Committee.

When does the SACAA Aeromedical Committee meet?

+

SACAA procedure AM007 says the committee meets on the third Tuesday of each month, and documents must be submitted at least seven days before the meeting.

How long are my medical examination results valid for?

+

Regulation 67.00.12 says examination records may be used to issue a certificate for no more than 90 days. After that the examination may need to be repeated.

How long do I have to appeal a medical decision?

+

SACAA procedure AM007 says you may appeal to the Director of Civil Aviation within 30 days. An appeal against a suspension must be made within 14 days, under regulation 67.00.14.

Can I fly while taking medicine?

+

Not while you know of a medicine or condition that could affect your certificate. Regulation 67.00.9 also requires you to report regular use of medicine. Ask your DAME before you fly.
